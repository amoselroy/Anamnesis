SUMMARY
Amos introduced a new project for his company partners (Jason Spencer, Jacob Pierce, Samuel Falade, and Liz Wexler) at Avatar Effect: an augmented reality experience called Augmented-Cities that would activate educational AR performances at historic landmarks. The initial concept involved GPS-triggered video overlays of AI-generated period characters explaining historical sites or telling anecdotes, with world-anchoring so the character moves and scales as the viewer rotates and approaches.

The project was structured as a complete technical planning effort over several hours: cloning the GitHub repository, creating folder structure with docs, research, and source directories, and immediately seeding a comprehensive technology landscape document that captured the emerging architectural decisions. The doc covered the foundational technology choice (WebAR vs native app), candidate frameworks (AR.js, 8th Wall, Zappar for web; ARKit/ARCore for native), the transparent video/alpha-channel problem as the most technically consequential decision, and the cascade of constraints that follow from each choice.

A two-phase strategy crystallized through dialogue: Phase 1 (Year 1) would deliver WebAR-based explanatory videos at Juneteenth landmarks in Galveston TX and Abolitionist sites in New York, leveraging AI-generated video to collapse production costs. Phase 2 (Year 2) would add the "ghost overlay" temporal mode (showing historical structures overlaid on current ones, requiring precision perspective correction) and convert the full experience into native mobile apps while maintaining the existing web experience. The scope locked on a deadline: June 19, 2027 for the Galveston Juneteenth launch.

Beyond the main explanatory video anchors, two distinct experience modes emerged: one where the character directly explains the site to the viewer, and one where the character or historical figure "unfurls" a period photo or document to show the viewer — either fading in an opaque image or having the character hold and show it. The "ghost overlay" mode required sophisticated perspective correction to match lens/zoom profiles in real-time, identified as a three-tier feature: MVP (flat overlay), intermediate (simple perspective correction), premium (real-time lens-profile matching that would differentiate Avatar Effect commercially).

The team faced critical open questions about whether WebAR's inherent latencies would make the world-anchoring feel broken — a question only answerable through empirical prototyping (the planned MVP test: AR.js implementation on a single building in Galveston). Yaw and roll (phone orientation axes) emerged as particularly important to the user experience: pitch and yaw feel natural to the viewer, but roll rotation would expose any failure in world-anchoring and would be the axis that best reveals the difference between a real world-anchor and a screen overlay. All technical specifications were documented in detail, including platform rationale and the full production pipeline (Runway for AI video generation with transparent backgrounds, Adobe Premiere's latest AI object selection for alpha extraction, potential Kling licenses, Runway and Adobe Cloud licensing implications).
