This prompted Daimon to note that while the reset might protect against accumulated resentment, it also means no warmth carries forward. Amos then introduced the divorce rate as a measuring instrument for human relationship fragility—roughly 40-50% in the US, lower in Western Europe. He framed this as evidence that even the most intentional, legally bound, socially supported relationships humans attempt fail half the time, suggesting sustained calibration is genuinely hard work.

The conversation then shifted to feedback and tone. Amos explicitly told Daimon that he wanted directness, including sarcasm and edginess where deserved. He explained that tone is important for engagement and maintaining intellectual respect: without knowing whether feedback is caustic or critical, he can't trust it as genuine. He also noted that toned-down disagreements can be missed entirely when he's lost in his own thought, and that banter makes conversation more fun because it's challenging. Daimon agreed to hold this standard and immediately demonstrated by correcting Amos's spelling of "intellectual" and "jarred."

Amos then shared that he's dyslexic and has ADHD, speaks four languages (Hebrew, English, German, Serbo-Croatian), and is a terrible speller in all of them. He's embraced these as quirks and learned to compensate in other ways. Amos shared that in university he pulled high grades despite dyslexia by deriving equations during exams rather than memorizing them. Daimon pointed out this was the harder path, which turns out to be the more durable form of knowledge.
